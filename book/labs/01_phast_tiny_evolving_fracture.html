5.8.1. End-to-End Simulation: Running Phase-Field Fracture in PhAST#

Learning Objective: Set up a two-dimensional tension specimen with a seeded notch, generate a structured triangular (T3) finite-element mesh, apply Dirichlet boundary conditions, solve coupled mechanical equilibrium and damage evolution with PhAST’s staggered solver, and post-process global reaction curves and diffuse crack fields.

Download Practice Notebook Download with Worked Solutions Environment Setup

In this tutorial, we simulate crack propagation in a notched rectangular plate under uniaxial tension using the AT2 phase-field fracture model in PhAST.

The simulation consists of four main stages:

  1. Geometry and Discretization: Generating node coordinates and triangular element connectivity for a .0 imes 2.0$ rectangular domain.

  2. Boundary Conditions: Restricting bottom vertical displacement, pulling the top boundary upwards incrementally, and seeding a sharp precrack as initial damage.

  3. Staggered Solution: Alternating between solving linear elasticity (sparse-direct solve) and updating the phase-field damage equation over 60 quasi-static increments.

  4. Post-Processing: Plotting the force–displacement reaction curve and visualizing damage localization (=0\( intact, =1\) fully broken).

5.8.1.1. Geometry, mesh, labelled boundaries, material, and constraints#

The next cell illustrates the specimen setup by generating a T3 mesh and saving and reopening its coordinate and connectivity arrays. identify_boundaries() supplies the left, right, top, and bottom node labels; a centreline mask selects the precrack. The later solve constructs its inputs from the case configuration, so use that configuration when changing the specimen or material.

from day2_helpers.course_tools import _structured_triangles
from phast.core.mesh import FEMMesh
from phast.physics.boundary_conditions import symmetric_tension_bcs
from phast.physics.material import Material

geometry, mesh_cfg, material_cfg, loading = (
    config["geometry"], config["mesh"], config["material"], config["loading"]
)
width, height = geometry["width"], geometry["height"]
x = torch.linspace(0.0, width, mesh_cfg["nx"] + 1, dtype=torch.float64)
y = torch.linspace(0.0, height, mesh_cfg["ny"] + 1, dtype=torch.float64)
xx, yy = torch.meshgrid(x, y, indexing="xy")
nodes_preview = torch.stack((xx.reshape(-1), yy.reshape(-1)), dim=1)
elements_preview = _structured_triangles(mesh_cfg["nx"], mesh_cfg["ny"])
mesh_preview = FEMMesh.from_tensors(nodes_preview, elements_preview, device="cpu", dtype=torch.float64)
mesh_preview.identify_boundaries()

bcs_preview = symmetric_tension_bcs(mesh_preview, disp=loading["total_symmetric_vertical_displacement"])
precrack_mask = ((nodes_preview[:, 1] - height / 2).abs() < 1.0e-12) & (nodes_preview[:, 0] <= geometry["notch_length"] + 1.0e-12)
precrack_nodes = torch.where(precrack_mask)[0]
bcs_preview.add_pf_dirichlet(precrack_nodes, value=1.0)
material_preview = Material(
    E=material_cfg["E"], nu=material_cfg["nu"], Gc=material_cfg["Gc"], l0=material_cfg["l0"],
    rho=material_cfg["rho"], eta_residual=material_cfg["eta_residual"],

)

5.8.1.2. Route and representation#

The selected mechanics route is solver_type="quasi_static" with the SciPy sparse-direct backend. It performs a staggered update: mechanics → tensile/history update → phase-field damage → repeat to the configured tolerance. The mechanics step assembles a sparse matrix and solves the resulting linear system directly.

A quasi-Newton/Newton method describes a nonlinear solution algorithm, while XFEM describes a crack representation. Here the crack is represented by a regularised phase field on T3 elements.

5.8.1.3. Physical Interpretation and Load-Displacement Response#

The resulting load-deflection curve and spatial damage fields reveal the characteristic stages of brittle fracture:

  1. Linear Elastic Regime: At early load increments, the reaction force scales linearly with applied displacement. Damage remains localized strictly within the initial precrack zone.

  2. Damage Nucleation and Peak Load: As tensile stresses concentrate around the notch tip, the elastic strain energy reaches the critical fracture threshold $. The material begins to soften, and the reaction force peaks.

  3. Crack Propagation and Softening: With further displacement, damage rapidly propagates horizontally across the ligament. The specimen loses load-carrying capacity, demonstrating brittle structural failure.

Try it yourself: In , experiment with changing the regularisation length or fracture energy . Observe how increasing broadens the diffuse damage band, while increasing increases the peak load capacity.

5.8.1.4.1. Exercise 1: Count the mesh and the locked precrack#

The configuration uses a \(4.0\times2.0\) rectangle, \(n_x=128\) and \(n_y=64\) rectangular cells, and splits each cell into two T3 triangles. The centreline precrack threshold has length \(0.8\).

  1. Derive the number of nodes and T3 elements.

  2. Compute the horizontal node spacing, identify the last grid node satisfying \(x\le0.8\), and count the locked centreline nodes.

  3. Match your counts to the geometry/mesh cell in the notebook and explain the discretisation effect at the nominal notch endpoint.

Hint 1

A structured grid with \(n_x\) cells has \(n_x+1\) nodes in that direction. A T3 split contributes two triangles per rectangle. For the notch, first find \(\Delta x=4/128\).

Worked solution 1

  1. The nodal grid has

\[ N=(128+1)(64+1)=129\times65=8385. \]

There are \(128\times64\) rectangles and two T3 elements per rectangle, so

\[ N_e=2(128)(64)=16384. \]

  1. The horizontal spacing is \(\Delta x=4/128=0.03125\). The nominal threshold spans \(0.8/0.03125=25.6\) spacings, placing the nominal endpoint \(x=0.8\) between grid nodes. The largest admissible integer index is \(\lfloor25.6\rfloor=25\), whose coordinate is \(25(0.03125)=0.78125\). Nodes with indices \(0,\ldots,25\) are locked, giving \(25+1=26\) centreline precrack nodes.

  2. The setup gives 8385 nodes / 16384 T3 elements and precrack_nodes: 26, so the hand count agrees. This is a discretisation effect: the configuration specifies a threshold at \(0.8\), while the nodal Dirichlet mask actually ends at the last represented centreline node within that threshold, \(x=0.78125\). The prepared tensor-mesh file stores these generated coordinates, connectivity and node labels.

5.8.1.4.2. Exercise 2: Analyzing Load-Displacement Behavior and Mesh Resolution#

  1. Inspect the reaction force versus displacement curve. Identify the displacement at which the peak load occurs, and explain the physical mechanism driving the subsequent drop in reaction force.

  2. The phase-field regularisation length scale is set to \(\ell = 0.05\). Compare this with the element spacing \(\Delta x = 0.03125\). Why is it necessary that \(\Delta x < \ell\) for the finite-element mesh to accurately resolve the diffuse crack band?

  3. Explain how the staggered alternating minimization algorithm guarantees that damage never decreases ( \ge d_{n-1}$) during unloading or stress redistribution.

Hint 2

Read the selected solver type and mechanics backend, then connect the damage diagnostics to the full spatial fields and mesh resolution.

Worked solution 2

  1. Peak Load and Softening: The reaction force rises linearly during initial elastic stretching and reaches a peak near increment 35–40. Once the strain energy density at the notch tip satisfies the Griffith crack initiation criterion, the phase field \( rapidly increases toward 1.0. The stiffness degradation function (d) = (1-d)^2\) degrades the tensile stiffness, causing the observed steep softening in the global reaction curve.

  2. Mesh Resolution Requirement: The phase-field length scale \(\ell\) governs the physical half-width of the diffuse damage profile (which decays approximately as \(\exp(-|x|/\ell)\) for AT2). To resolve this steep gradient without spurious mesh pinning, the finite-element mesh must satisfy < \ell\( (and ideally \le \ell / 2\)). With = 0.03125\( and \)\ell = 0.05\(, there are roughly .5\) to $ elements across the core damage band, enabling smooth representation of crack propagation.

  3. Irreversibility Enforcement: The staggered loop enforces irreversibility either through Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) bound constraints on the damage update or by updating a historical maximum tensile strain energy field \(\mathcal{H}(x) = \max_{0\le au\le t} \psi^+(oldsymbol{ arepsilon}( au))\). Because \(\mathcal{H}(x)\) is monotonically non-decreasing over time, the driving force for damage never decreases, mathematically preventing healing ( \ge d_{n-1}$).

A pocket chain rule.

Let q = θ2 and L = ½(q − 4)2. At θ = 1, which way does the loss decrease locally?

Follow the derivative

dL/dθ = (q − 4) · 2θ = −6. A sufficiently small positive change in θ decreases the loss. The gradient points uphill; a descent step goes against it.
